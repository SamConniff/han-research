01

Does it track?

Short answer: yes on all three, and the audience read is the strongest of them. One thing needs fixing before any of it gets made.

Holds

The insight

"The patriarchy is the system that holds men back too" is a genuinely punk proposition — it reframes the accused as the constrained. Punk's whole grammar is refusing an assigned role. Painting your nails is a small, cheap, public refusal. The aesthetic isn't decoration on that idea; it's the same idea in a different medium.

Holds, strongly

The audience

You've heard it directly and repeatedly: I used to paint my nails when I was a punk / a goth / a new romantic. That isn't a nice coincidence, it's a route back in. A 45–58-year-old firefighter didn't lose the capacity for self-decoration; he was told to put it away. The 1978–1990 sleeve language is the visual proof that he once had permission — and it belongs to him, not to a salon.

Holds, with a condition

The opportunity

The badge is the opportunity, and Emma just proved it: she asked to put the mark on her own signature before anyone asked her to. That's the whole test. But a badge people want to wear at work has to survive being seen by their boss, their client, and their kid — which is exactly where the finger becomes a live question rather than a style choice.

The one thing I'd change

Danielly is right that the middle finger is too aggressive, and wrong about why — which means his conclusion (soften it) is the wrong fix. Aggression isn't in the gesture. It's in the aim. A finger pointed down the lens is aimed at the viewer: it says fuck you to a man who has done nothing yet except look. The same hand raised to the side, to camera-left, aimed at nothing in the frame, reads as fuck this — at the rule, not the reader.

Every gesture in this system points at the system, never at the audience. That single rule lets you keep all of the attitude and lose all of the hostility, and it's the reason both Danielly and Emma can be right at the same time.

02

The salute

You already have a middle finger in the Hard As Nails wordmark, going into the A. Don't touch it. But it can't also be the thing a hundred men do in a hundred photographs — it's a mark, not a pose, and a mark repeated a hundred times stops being a mark.

→ You

Aimed at the viewer

Straight down the lens, centred, filling the frame. Reads as contempt for whoever is looking. This is what Danielly is reacting to, and he's not wrong — a partner-facing surface can't open with an insult to a stranger.

→ It

Aimed at the system

Raised to the side, off-axis, thrown at something outside the crop. Same defiance, different target. The viewer becomes a co-conspirator rather than the object. This is the shootable rule — put it in the photographer's brief in one line.

Rare · owned · one place

The finger stays in the logo

  • It's already there, it's earned, and it predates all of this.
  • It appears once, in the mark, at brand level — never in participant photography, never on a partner deck.
  • Scarcity is what keeps it a signal instead of a tic. One joke told once is a mark; told a hundred times it's a mannerism.
  • Keep your email signature exactly as it is. The gentleman-illustration-flipping-the-bird works precisely because it's yours and singular. It is not a system, and it shouldn't be asked to become one.
Repeatable · British · groupable

The V is the people's gesture

  • Participant portraits, email signatures, badges, stickers, the partner pack — the V is the shareable one.
  • It's specifically British and specifically cheeky rather than furious. It carries mischief, which is the register your participants already write in.
  • It photographs better in a grid: two fingers make a legible silhouette at 40px, a single finger reads as a smudge.
  • It shows more nails. Two painted nails to camera instead of one — the product is literally more visible.
  • Emma can put it on her signature on Monday. That's the point of it.
03

Two colours

The discipline that makes this look like 1979 rather than like a brand guideline isn't the colours themselves — it's the constraint. Punk sleeves were two-colour because two-colour was cheap. Malcolm Garrett reduced Linder's full-colour montage for Orgasm Addict to two colours purely to cut the print bill, and that economy is the look. Adopt the constraint and the aesthetic comes free.

Ground#0A0709

Warm near-black, not #000. Everything sits on it. Never a white page with black type — invert the default and half the work is done.

Rhodamine#E6007E

Already live on the research site. It's process magenta — the ink, not a tint. Full strength or not at all; a 40% pink is a nursery.

Hot#FF338F

Screen-only lift for links, hovers and states. Doesn't exist in print. Keep it out of any artwork brief.

Paper#F3ECEF

Off-white with a pink bias, so the whites look printed rather than lit. Pure #FFF only in the wordmark.

Smiley#E4FF3D

Third ink, rationed. A participant described his own nails as "neon pink and 'smiley' yellow" — the audience handed you the acid-house accent unprompted. Use it for one thing only: alerts, deadlines, the campaign month.

04

Rescuing the hundred

You don't need to reshoot a hundred men. The photographs aren't twee because of the nails — they're twee because of the light and the posture. Soft light, white background, apologetic smile, hand held out for inspection like a swatch. That's the visual grammar of a salon, and it's doing the opposite of what the research says.

No reshoot · this week

Treat what you already have

  1. Duotone everything: black and rhodamine, two plates, no third colour, no greys that aren't made of dots.
  2. Coarse halftone — visibly dotted, the way a 1979 sleeve was. Grain kills preciousness faster than any art direction.
  3. Crop tight and crop hard. Hands and faces only. Kitchens, sofas and salon interiors are where the tweeness lives.
  4. Grid them. One man is a portrait; a hundred men in a 10 × 10 wall is a movement, and the repetition does the argument for you.
  5. Keep the WhatsApp screengrabs untreated as counterpoint. Their rawness is the evidence layer — Central Station collaged found material, and yours is better because it's genuinely yours.
2026 campaign · shoot brief

Shoot the next hundred properly

  1. Hard direct flash, black ground, low horizon. Shoot a fireman like a monument, not like a client — see Brian Griffin's A Broken Frame.
  2. No smiling for approval. Deadpan or defiant. A smile is fine if it's his, not one offered to the camera.
  3. Hands as fists, Vs, and grips — never displayed palm-down for inspection.
  4. Shoot in the uniform, the hi-vis, the kit. The collision of workwear and painted nails is the entire story in one frame; a plain t-shirt throws it away.
  5. Every gesture aimed off-frame. The rule from section 02, in the call sheet.

Lane A

Photomontage as argument

The direct ancestor. Cut-up bodies and domestic objects, used to attack gender roles, printed in two colours because that's all anyone could afford.

Orgasm Addict

Buzzcocks · New Hormones · 1977
Montage: Linder Sterling · Design: Malcolm Garrett

An iron-headed nude with mouths for nipples, spliced from pornography and homes-and-gardens magazines. Garrett stripped it to two colours to save money. It's in MoMA's collection.

Take — the method, not the shock. A feminist photomontage about gendered bodies, on a punk sleeve, in black and one hot colour. This is your reference of record.

Penis Envy

Crass · Crass Records · 1981
Artwork: Gee Vaucher

Vaucher's photomontage and gouache work for Crass — forensic, monochrome, stencilled, and explicitly about gender and conditioning rather than nihilism.

Take — the seriousness. Proof that a graphic can be angry and rigorous at once. Also the stencil system: one repeatable mark, applied everywhere.

Lane B

The sleeve carries the data

Where the artwork is the manifesto. You have nine-month empathy scores and a control group. Almost nobody in this category has evidence — put it on the merch.

Two Tribes

Frankie Goes To Hollywood · ZTT · 1984
Design: XL/ZTT · Photography: Anton Corbijn · Text: Paul Morley

Morley loaded the sleeve with copy, including a chart of the biological effects of nuclear fallout on civilians and a breakdown of US and Soviet arsenals. A pop single that shipped with a data appendix.

Take — the exact move for Hard As Nails. MIAB scores, the control group, empathy at nine months — set as sleeve notes on the back of a poster, not as a PDF nobody opens.

Art of ZTT →

Red Mecca / 2x45 / Micro-Phonies

Cabaret Voltaire · Fetish & Some Bizzare · 1981–84
Design: Neville Brody

Brody's Fetish period, then The Face from 1981. Everything hand-made — painted, carved, PMT'd, pasted from film overlays, years before a computer touched it. He built bespoke symbols and used them as a language.

Take — a proprietary symbol set for Hard As Nails. Ten nails, a V, a hazard mark, a tick — drawn once, used as punctuation everywhere. Cheaper than illustration and infinitely more ownable.

Blue Monday (FAC 73)

New Order · Factory · 1983
Design: Peter Saville

A die-cut sleeve imitating a 5.25" floppy disk, with the title and catalogue number encoded as a colour code you can only decipher using the wheel printed on the back of Power, Corruption & Lies.

Take — withholding as confidence. Not everything explains itself. A brand that makes you lean in reads as adult, and adult is the antidote to twee.

Lane C

Neon collage, 1988–1992

The bullseye for your age range. Saturated, found, working-class, unembarrassed. This is where "a bit neon, a bit collage" actually lives.

Pills 'n' Thrills and Bellyaches

Happy Mondays · Factory FACT 320 · 1990
Design: Central Station Design

First pressings collaged real US candy wrappers — Factory flew Central Station to LA for a week to collect the raw material. Later pressings had to strip the logos out for copyright.

Take — the tone above all: joyful, blokey, zero art-school apology. And the warning: collage your own found material. Yours is the WhatsApp group, and it's better because nobody can take it off you.

Pop Will Eat Itself · RCA · 1989
Design: The Designers Republic

Sheffield. Pseudo-corporate marks, radiation and hazard symbols, fake official language deployed as a joke on official language. Held by the V&A.

A Broken Frame

Depeche Mode · Mute · 1982
Photography: Brian Griffin · Design: Martyn Atkins

A woman scything corn under a vast storm sky, shot with the visual language of Soviet socialist realism. Life named it one of the best photographs of the 1980s.

Take — the whole answer to twee. Griffin photographed ordinary labour as monumental. Do that to a fire-service watch manager with pink nails and nobody will call it embarrassing.

Violator

Depeche Mode · Mute · 1990
Design and photography: Anton Corbijn

One red bloom on a black field and nothing else. The record inside is about desire and shame; the sleeve says it without a figure or a word of explanation.

Take — the restraint, and the proof that one saturated colour on black can carry an emotional argument on its own. Plus the rest of Corbijn's run: blown-out grain, hard contrast, subjects who look like they'd rather be elsewhere. Never flattering, always dignifying — permission for imperfection.

06

What it isn't

Your exclusions, sharpened — because each of these is what a designer will drift toward the moment the brief says "nails".

Nail salon

No script logotypes, no dusty-rose gradients, no bottle-and-brush photography, no hand held out for inspection. The nail is a mark, not a manicure.

Camp

Warmth and humour, yes — that's in the participants' own words. Knowingness and wink, no. The joke is on the rule, never on the men.

Vintage retro

Not a pastiche of 1979. Take the method — two plates, hand-cut, coarse halftone — and use it on 2026 photographs of 2026 men.

Full New Romantic

No frills, no ruffs, no Adam Ant stripe. Blitz-adjacent means the graphics, not the costume — the men in this study wear hi-vis.

Ransom note

Cut-out lettering is punk's most exhausted signifier. Cut up the images, set the type straight.

Charity

No soft-focus testimonial photography, no muted palette, no rounded corners. You have a control group. Look like research, behave like a movement.

07

Three true things

Usable on a poster, in a keynote, or on the back of the partner pack. Two are gifts. One is a landmine you should know about before someone hands it to you.

420 BC

The middle finger is 2,400 years old. Aristophanes puts it in The Clouds, where a character uses it to mock Socrates. Rome called it digitus impudicus, the indecent digit. It is older than every stereotype we're asking men to drop.

1918

Pink was a boys' colour. Earnshaw's Infants' Department, June 1918: "the generally accepted rule is pink for the boys, and blue for the girls… pink, being a more decided and stronger colour, is more suitable for the boy." Documented by Jo B. Paoletti. The thing we're asking men to reclaim was assigned to them within living memory of their grandfathers.

1415

Don't use the Agincourt story. The tale that English archers invented the V-sign after the French threatened to cut off their bow fingers is a myth — no chronicle records it, and it only became popular in the 1990s. The British Library finds no link. If you want a real date, the V-sign appears in the Macclesfield Psalter around 1330, eighty-odd years before the battle.

08

What I'd do next

Settle the gesture in one line

Send Danielly and Emma the aim rule from section 02 — at the system, never at the viewer. It resolves their disagreement without either of them losing, and it's a rule a photographer can actually follow.

Treat twenty of the existing hundred

Duotone, coarse halftone, hard crop. Twenty is enough to know whether the treatment rescues the library or whether you're reshooting. A day's work, and it answers the expensive question first.

Draw the symbol set

Ten nails, the V, a hazard mark, a tick. Brody's approach: a small proprietary alphabet used as punctuation everywhere. This is what makes Partners Now feel like the same organism as the research site.

Decide the Partners Now dial

The punk lives at brand and movement level. Partner-facing surfaces carry it in the colour, the type and the edit — not in a gesture aimed at somebody's HR director. Same world, lower volume. Worth agreeing explicitly before anyone designs a screen.

Tell me about Pumpkin Rebellion

You mentioned it twice and I don't have enough to place it. If it's a campaign moment, it may want its own colour — the smiley yellow is currently unspent.
